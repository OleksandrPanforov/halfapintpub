Today I'm going to write about the horrible, filthy phenomenon in our shared hobby: money. A catastrophe, really, since in polite society it's only whispered about in the kitchen, between counting other people's dollars.

As a GM, I've run both paid and free games. As a player, I've played in both, too, but this time I'll focus more on the GM's perspective.

In my experience, the difference comes down to expectations, and depends a lot on how well the GM knows the group.

I've decided that running paid games isn't for me, since the fee rarely fully covers the preparation.


But let's take this in order.

## Paid Games: How and Why?

<div style="text-align: center;">

**They encourage the GM to take the game seriously.**

**They make it possible to invest more in the process.**

**They can have a negative effect on friendships.**

**They provide simple, straightforward motivation.**

</div>


<figure>
    <img src="/images/alexander-mils-lCPhGxs7pww-unsplash.jpg"
         alt="A person counting a fan of hundred-dollar bills">
    <figcaption><span style="font-size:0.8em;"><i>Save this photo to your blog, and your players will bring you money for the next game. The author accepts no responsibility for unrealistic expectations.</i></span></figcaption>
</figure>


I've never run games as my main job. So the experience below comes from running paid games as a second, third, or fourth job, or observing other GMs.


I'll split this category in two, based on how well the GM knows the players. Overall, it's about how you position yourself. You charge money? You must be a great GM.

I'll note up front that I'm considering almost ideal cases, where neither the GM nor the group is trying to gain an advantage at the table because of the money involved. Slipping someone a hundred under the table to make sure an attack hits, or threatening to ask for a refund unless the GM stops seducing kobolds, is a different problem.


### Paid Games with Players You Don't Know

Players are more likely to show up expecting the GM to run a fantastic game. That makes sense: the GM is charging money.

At the same time, the GM may expect the players to be reasonable, since they know what they're paying for. And the GM gets a financial incentive, on top of whatever else motivates them to run these games.

One advantage is that this kind of commercial relationship clearly distinguishes what each side brings to the game. The GM prepares, investing time and effort to make the game excellent - trying to create a product people will want to buy. The other players purchase a quality product rather than one of the free alternatives available.

I'm not saying that free games are poor quality, or that paid games are always fun. These are expectations that may or may not be met. For both sides, paying for a game is a bit like a membership fee to a private club. In other words, there's a cost of entry.

However, it can lead to GM burnout if they try too hard. Or the campaign can devolve into "whoever pays calls the tune," with everything else lost in an attempt to please the players.


### Paid Games with Friends

It's almost the same as playing with strangers, but here it's more about the group understanding that the GM puts significantly more time and effort into the game than the other players do.

It's not about buying a gaming service so much as compensating the GM for the time they've spent. A thank-you in monetary form. Or pizza, drinks, snacks - whatever works.

Another benefit is that it's easier to communicate expectations for the game openly. I mean that the dynamic of the GM knowing the group better works less well here. Everyone has contributed to a great story - some with money, some with time. That involvement gives everyone a kind of say, and a desire to use it.

This makes preparation easier for the GM, since they know what the group wants. The problem of overpreparing is also less pronounced: if the group wants to befriend wolves, you don't need to prepare stat blocks for them.

Still, charging friends can be hard, especially when you know about their financial situation. And money rarely helps a friendship, so the downside here is mostly social.


### A Few More Things

Paid games give the GM extra financial options, regardless of the group.

You don't have to think about how much of your own (or your family's) budget to spend on adventures, rulebooks, printing materials, or ordering miniatures. Earn a thousand from games, put it into developing the campaign. Don't earn it, don't invest it - write a new campaign instead. It's motivating, helpful, inspiring. But not for everyone.

At the same time, a campaign can devolve into a string of filler episodes, with the GM running games for as long as they're being paid. The story may never reach its conclusion, because eventually players lose interest in continuing to buy the same old "one fight, one social encounter, three conversations, see you next week" format.


A separate category of GMs run games for donations to various worthy causes. I'm grateful to these people, because they're doing amazing work. I want to remind them to take care of themselves and remember: you can't collect every donation. If you burn out, games will become nothing but repulsive, and then there won't be any donations at all.


## Free Games: Why Bother?

<div style="text-align: center;">

**They suit people who are passionate about the hobby and have enough resources to make it happen.**

**Far fewer obligations; nobody owes anybody anything.**

**They need a different kind of motivation.**

**They can be intimate games with friends.**

</div>

<figure>
    <img src="/images/jp-valery-blOLCO2K4M0-unsplash.jpg"
         alt="A person standing on a rocky summit above the clouds">
    <figcaption><span style="font-size:0.8em;"><i>Sometimes you have enough time and money to simply spend time on your hobby. Let the whole world burn; you've got more pressing things to do.</i></span></figcaption>
</figure>


It's the same division as before, based on how well you know your players. Overall, it's still about positioning yourself. No financial motivation? Run games for free.

The cases I'm describing here are almost textbook examples, where everyone at the table does their best even though the game is free, while the GM might "cut corners." Or perhaps they don't try at all. As always, reality lies somewhere in between.


### Free Games with Strangers

They work well for players who have more time than money and prefer "no-strings-attached games."

It's a double-edged sword. In any dispute, both the GM and the players can say they're not paying for this and don't owe anyone anything. A player doesn't have to read their character sheet, the GM doesn't have to prepare maps, everyone can show up late, or behave like [That Guy](https://www.urbandictionary.com/define.php?term=That%20Guy) - after all, they're not paying.

It's a great way for a GM to gain a broad range of experience, because open games can attract all kinds of people. The game is available to anyone with enough free time. So be ready to learn conflict management, how to deal with bullying, how to discover hidden talents, and a range of other skills you never wanted but ended up acquiring.

At the same time, you need serious motivation, since money isn't part of the picture: becoming a better GM, building a community, bringing in new players, and so on.


### Free Games with Friends

They're more intimate.

The GM still prepares and puts in time and effort, so what's in it for them?

You just get together to have a good time, and the reason is obvious: to play your favorite game with friends. It's entirely possible that the group takes turns GMing, which is a great way to keep one person from getting too burned out.

One unexpected downside is that some groups of friends get together just to get together, not to play. The further we get into the magical world of life after thirty, the more often that happens.

At first glance, everything seems great. But if you're a GM used to running a plot and an epic narrative, the players may spend their time together chatting, whether or not the plot is happening in the background.

Also, trust tends to last longer among friends, which is a plus. But an outside conflict can spill into the game or affect the party's composition.


### A Few More Things

Running games for free is great, but it can be draining. Without a "backup" financial motivation, it's hard to keep putting the same effort into a game.

And life happens. Players may deprioritize a free game, especially if you don't know each other very well.

Of course, any improvements to the experience - printing materials, buying rulebooks, and so on - are on the GM. It's great when you can afford that, but it can affect your decision.

Or you can chip in with friends for the rulebook of a new system everyone is curious about, then take turns reading it.


## What Have I Decided for Myself?

<div style="text-align: center;">

**Passion first, money second.**

**Work out how much time and money go into preparation. Turn the players' payments into an hourly rate.**

**Get discouraged by the numbers, never look at them again, and hope it might become profitable someday.**

**As long as I have the time and opportunity, this works for me.**

</div>

<figure>
    <img src="/images/pascal-habermann-pqHRNS8Mojc-unsplash.jpg"
         alt="A hand holding burning dollar bills">
    <figcaption><span style="font-size:0.8em;"><i>You've reached the end of the post, where there was supposed to be an answer to a simple question, but now you're standing here alone, wondering what to do.</i></span></figcaption>
</figure>


I once heard that the secret to success is: "Passion first, money second."

It makes sense here, too, because in my opinion, the process is what matters most in games. The fun, the good times, the relaxation, and the stories the group will tell for years afterward. You can monetize that, sure. But then the process of monetization becomes the main thing.


Let's try to do the math.


<div style="text-align: center;">
<i>(Number of players * game fee)/game length in hours = hourly rate</i>
</div>
<br>


If that makes you angry - congratulations, you've cleared the first circle.


Let's take a standard group of four players, since more can get difficult.

Let's charge €6.34 per player, a little above average, because we're determined to make our first million from games.

Let's say the game lasts four hours, so we can run two in a day.


<div style="text-align: center;">
<i>(4 * €6.34)/4 = €6.34/hour</i>
</div>
<br>


Sounds good, doesn't it? With six players, it would be €9.51/hour! Or €76.05 for two games on a Saturday. I think you get the math.

But this formula doesn't account for preparation time.


<div style="text-align: center;">
<i>(Number of players * game fee)/(game length + preparation time) = hourly rate</i>
</div>


Let's use all the same numbers as in the previous example, except for preparation time.

In my experience, preparing a published adventure takes one to two hours for every hour of play. Let's use two, just to be safe.


<div style="text-align: center;">
<i>(4 * €6.34)/(4 + 8) = €2.11/hour</i>
</div>

Oof. That's not so great anymore. Even with six players, it's only €3.17.


This formula may also need to include:

- the fee for the venue (club, anti-cafe, and so on);

- travel costs to get to the venue;

- the cost of rulebooks, adventures, and other materials;

- maps, miniatures, handouts;

- coffee, lunch at the venue;

- time spent finding players;

- other expenses specific to your game.


And then the hourly rate can easily drop to €0.38–€0.63. For context, at the time of writing, you could find promoter work in Kyiv for €2.54/hour, working three hours a day. But handing out flyers isn't the same as running a game.


#### What Can You Do?

+ prepare one adventure and run it many times;

+ use supporting resources to help with preparation;

+ run longer games that need less preparation;

+ play online and avoid some of the expenses listed above.


The idea is to try to account for everything as fully as possible and figure out what works.


### And Now I'll Add My Own Experience

I get bored of running the same adventure more than three or four times. So preparation time is unavoidable. I enjoy preparing at least as much as playing, but that's my guilty pleasure.

For the homebrew games I love running in person, I can easily spend four or five hours preparing for every hour of play.

Because I count all of this:

+ writing the plot;

+ finding 3D miniature models, printing and painting them;

+ printing maps and materials;

+ creating handouts and plot elements;

+ drawing maps in [Dungeondraft](https://dungeondraft.net/);

+ plot-specific stuff.


#### Additional Expenses?

+ system rulebooks and other materials from [DriveThruRPG](http://drivethrurpg.com/);

+ 3D models for printing;

+ printing supplies;

+ a 3D printer;

+ plot-specific stuff.




Yes, all of this took time to put together, and it's the result of a long, painstaking effort. In this case, the hourly rate is so tiny that I feel awkward asking players for money. Not only would they be unable to reimburse my expenses, they never asked me to do things this way in the first place. Would I do it differently? Only under different circumstances.


For me, it's an exciting process, from grabbing my phone at three in the morning to jot down some obvious nonsense like "Here, time turns into space," to a player saying "Wow" after six hours of play. And since it's about the process, not the money, after trying different things, I decided to run less often, for free, but better and the way I want to.

### Why Does This Matter to Me?


If it means a player who wanted to try the game can't afford it - that happens to the best of us.

<div style="text-align: right;">
<i>But no lack of money will stop them from getting to the game whose time has come.</i>
</div>


If it means postponing a game because one of the players is sick, that means the others value playing with the whole group.

<div style="text-align: right;">
<i>And we can be glad once again that we survived the pandemic.</i>
</div>


If it means taking an eighteen-month break in the campaign, that's no big deal; we'll pick up where we left off.

<div style="text-align: right;">
<i>Or where we remember leaving off.</i>
</div>


If it means the next open-table game will be at the next DiceCon festival, that's fine.

<div style="text-align: right;">
<i>Because afterward, the players will have that spark in their eyes, and that's what matters.</i>
</div>


If it means putting off buying materials on [Kickstarter](https://www.kickstarter.com/discover/advanced?ref=nav_search&term=ttrpg) for a week or two, that's no big deal, even if I won't play them for another year.

<div style="text-align: center;">
<i>However you look at it, this hobby is about passion, which takes priority over money. Not the other way around.</i>

As usual, I'll try to sum up. Groups are different, GMs are different, circumstances are different, and I don't see a universal solution. At the same time:
